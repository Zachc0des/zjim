01 — the framework

Pick a goal, then let the numbers follow

Every rep range trains a different quality. Decide what you're chasing before you decide what to do — the sets and reps are just the delivery mechanism.

Strength

3–5
reps · 3–5 sets · rest 3–5 min

Heavy loads, near-maximal effort, long rests so you can move real weight again next set. Best for getting numbers up on the big lifts.

Muscle (hypertrophy)

8–12
reps · 3–4 sets · rest 60–90s

Moderate load, enough volume to fatigue the muscle, shorter rest to keep tension high across the session.

Endurance

15–20+
reps · 2–3 sets · rest 30–45s

Lighter load, higher output, minimal rest. Builds the muscular stamina that supports everything above it.

02 — the big lifts

Five compound movements, one whole body

Compound lifts move more than one joint at a time, so they recruit the most muscle for the least time spent. Build your sessions around these first — everything else is an accessory to them.

Squat

Form cue

Sit back and down like reaching for a low chair. Keep your chest tall and drive through the whole foot, not just the toes, on the way up.

Push the floor away with your legs while keeping the bar brushing your shins. Flat back, braced core, hips and shoulders rise together.

Form cue

Shoulder blades pinched and pinned to the bench. Lower the bar to mid-chest under control, then press it back in a slight arc, not straight up.

04

Form cue

Brace your core like you're about to be punched, then press straight up and slightly back so the bar finishes over your ears, not your face.

Form cue

Pull with your elbows, not your hands — imagine dragging them straight down past your ribs. Squeeze your shoulder blades together at the top.

03 — no gym, no problem

Same movement patterns, zero equipment

You don't need a barbell to train a pattern — you need resistance and a full range of motion. Bodyweight covers you until you can add load with a backpack, bands, or dumbbells.

Push-ups

Grip a sturdy table edge or towel wrapped around a door, lean back, and pull your chest to your hands.
Core Work
↓ becomes

Plank for total-body bracing, dead bug for control — reach opposite arm and leg out slowly without your back arching.
04 — engine work

Two speeds of cardio, two different jobs

Cardio isn't one thing. Slow and steady builds your base and speeds recovery; short and hard builds capacity and burns time efficiently. Most routines want both.

Zone 2 · LISS

Low intensity, steady state

Pace where you can hold a conversation. 30–60 minutes, 2–4x a week.

  • Brisk walking or hiking
  • Easy-pace cycling
  • Light jogging or swimming
HIIT

Short, hard intervals

Near-max effort for 20–40s, rest, repeat. 10–20 minutes total, 1–2x a week.

  • Sprint intervals
  • Jump rope rounds
  • Burpee or bike sprints

06 — before you load the bar

A five-minute warm-up sequence

Do this in order — it moves from general blood flow to the exact pattern you're about to load.

Raise your heart rate

60–90 seconds of light cardio — jumping jacks, a brisk walk, or rowing — to get blood moving to your muscles.

Move your joints through range

Arm circles, leg swings, hip openers, and torso twists — dynamic, not static holds.

Rehearse the pattern

A couple of bodyweight reps of whatever you're about to load — bodyweight squats before barbell squats, empty-bar presses before loaded ones.

Ramp the load

Work up in 2–3 warm-up sets toward your working weight so the first working set isn't your heaviest jump of the day.

07 — common mistakes

What derails most routines

Skipping the warm-up

Cold muscles under load is how minor tweaks become weeks off.

Chasing weight over form

A lighter, clean rep builds more than a heavy, sloppy one — and it keeps you training.

No progression plan

If you're not adding a little weight, a rep, or a set over time, your body has no reason to adapt.

Training through fatigue

Recovery is where the adaptation actually happens. Sleep and rest days are part of the program, not a break from it.
