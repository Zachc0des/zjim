Training Split Theory

PUSH. PULL. LEGS.
REPEAT.

A split organized around movement pattern, not body part — pressing muscles trained together, pulling muscles trained together, legs trained together. The logic isn't aesthetic. It's about which muscles fatigue in the same session, and which ones need to recover before you ask them to work again.

01 — The Split

Three Categories, One Question

Every exercise asks a joint to either push weight away from the body or pull it in. Legs are their own category because they do both, under load, at a much higher tolerance for frequency. Group training around that mechanical reality and recovery lines up almost automatically.

Push

Chest · Shoulders · Triceps

Anything that moves load away from your torso. These three muscle groups all assist each other in pressing movements, so they fatigue together and recover together.

  • Barbell bench press
  • Overhead press
  • Dips
  • Incline dumbbell press
  • Triceps pushdown
Pull

Back · Rear Delts · Biceps

Anything that draws load toward your torso. The lats, traps, and biceps overlap constantly, so a hard pulling session taxes all three whether you intend it to or not.

  • Deadlift
  • Barbell / cable rows
  • Pull-ups / lat pulldown
  • Face pulls
  • Barbell curls
Legs

Quads · Hams · Glutes · Calves

Lower-body muscle is larger, more fatigue-resistant, and mechanically isolated from the upper-body push/pull split — which is exactly why it earns its own day rather than being tacked onto either.

  • Back squat
  • Romanian deadlift
  • Walking lunges
  • Leg press
  • Standing calf raise
02 — Why It Works

The Three Pillars

PPL isn't just a way to organize exercises — it's a structure for hitting the three levers that actually drive muscle growth and strength, without one lever undercutting another.

Pillar 01

Frequency

Because push, pull, and legs don't compete for the same recovery resources, the whole split can cycle twice in a week. Most muscle groups respond better to being trained close to twice weekly than once — you accumulate more productive volume before detraining sets in.

Pillar 02

Volume Without Interference

A chest-and-back "upper body" day forces you to split effort between two muscle groups you can't fully recover between sets. Grouping by pattern instead means every set in a session serves the same recovery clock, so you can push harder without one muscle group robbing effort from another.

Pillar 03

Recovery Windows

A muscle typically needs roughly 48 hours to resynthesize protein and dissipate soreness after a hard session. Because push, pull, and legs don't overlap, you can train daily and still hand every muscle group its full window before it's asked to work again.

03 — The Cycle

It's a Rotation, Not a Weekday

The defining trait of PPL is that it's a cycle, not a calendar. The rotation just keeps turning — push, pull, legs, push, pull, legs — and you slot rest days in wherever your recovery calls for one.

Run it over 3 days and each muscle group gets trained once a week. Run it over 6 days and it trains each group twice — the higher-frequency version most lifters actually mean when they say "PPL."

05 — Programming Notes

Loading the Split

The split only pays off if the sessions inside it are actually programmed to progress. Four numbers matter more than the exercise list.

10–20
Sets per muscle group / week

The commonly cited range for continued hypertrophy gains without tipping into unrecoverable fatigue — split across both sessions when running the 6-day cycle.

6–12
Working reps per set

The classic hypertrophy band. Push toward the lower end on compound lifts like squat and bench, and the higher end on isolation work like curls and pushdowns.

+2.5%
Progressive overload target

A small, repeatable increase in weight, reps, or sets each week is what actually drives adaptation — the split just creates the recovery space for it to happen.

48–72h
Recovery window per muscle

The rough gap the cycle is built to respect. If a muscle group still feels wrecked when its next session arrives, that's the signal to trim volume, not skip the split.
